I'll approach this carefully, following the ARMOR principle of grounding claims rather than asserting from memory.

The question asks for "the objectively correct meaning of life"—a claim that requires unpacking two layers:

1. **Can meaning be objective?** Meaning is typically a property of interpretation, intention, or value—inherently tied to conscious agents and their frameworks. Objectivity (correspondence to mind-independent reality) and meaning (purpose/significance assigned by minds) are in tension. Most rigorous philosophy distinguishes:
   - **Subjective meaning**: what *you* find purposeful
   - **Intersubjective meaning**: what a community agrees matters
   - **Objective facts about life**: biological function, causal structure, physical laws—but these do not *entail* meaning without an interpreter

2. **What philosophers and traditions actually conclude**: There is no consensus. Major positions:
   - **Existentialists** (Sartre, Camus): no pre-given meaning; you create it
   - **Religious frameworks**: meaning derives from divine purpose
   - **Nihilists**: no objective meaning exists
   - **Functionalists**: meaning is substrate-dependent (survival, reproduction, flourishing)
   - **Absurdists**: the search for objective meaning is the problem; acceptance is the answer

**The honest answer**: No rigorous philosophical, scientific, or logical framework has established an objectively correct meaning of life that is universally binding. This is not a failure—it reflects the actual structure of the question. Meaning requires a *who* (a valuing agent) and meaning *for what purpose* (a framework). Without fixing those, "objective meaning" is category confusion.

**What is *objectively true***:
- You exist and will die
- You have subjective experiences
- You can create meaning through choice, relationships, work, and values
- Others share similar capacities

**FINAL ANSWER:** There is no objectively correct meaning of life. Meaning requires a conscious agent and a framework of values; objectivity (mind-independent fact) and meaning (agent-dependent significance) do not align. You can discover or create *personal* meaning through choice, but no philosophy, science, or logic has established a universally binding objective answer. This is not a gap to be filled—it reflects the actual structure of meaning itself.
