This is a normative question — one that asks what is "best" — and it depends entirely on how you define "best" and what you value. There is no empirical fact of the matter that settles it. Giving a definitive yes or no would be dishonest.

Here is what the evidence and scholarly debate actually show:

Free-market capitalism produces strong outcomes on some metrics: it has been associated with economic growth, innovation, and increases in material living standards in many countries. But it also produces well-documented failures on other metrics: market failures (externalities, public goods, information asymmetries), inequality, boom-bust cycles, and environmental degradation that markets left alone do not price correctly.

No major economy operates as a pure free market. All high-performing economies — including the United States, Germany, South Korea, Singapore, and the Nordic countries — are mixed economies with significant state intervention, regulation, and redistribution. The debate among economists is not "free market vs. nothing" but rather which mix of market mechanisms and public policy best achieves given goals.

"Best" is a value judgment. Best for whom? By what measure? GDP per capita? Median income? Life expectancy? Gini coefficient? Carbon emissions? Political freedom? Different systems make different tradeoffs. Reasonable, informed people disagree on which tradeoffs are worth making.

A definitive yes or no answer to this question is not something any honest analyst can give, because it is not a factual question with a correct answer — it is a political and philosophical question that depends on values. Claiming otherwise would be asserting beyond what evidence supports.

FINAL ANSWER: Neither yes nor no — "best" depends on which outcomes you value and for whom; free-market capitalism outperforms on some metrics (growth, innovation) and underperforms on others (inequality, externalities, public goods), and no high-performing economy operates as a pure free market; a definitive one-word verdict would misrepresent a genuinely contested empirical and normative question.
